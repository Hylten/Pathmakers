An owner decides to sell. The accounts are clean. Revenue has grown. EBITDA is strong. The business is profitable, and the owner knows every customer, solves every problem and makes every final decision. Due diligence begins. The buyer asks who holds the key relationships. The owner names himself. The buyer asks who can run the operation for thirty days without the owner. The owner hesitates. The offer changes.

This is the owner-dependency discount, and it is the most expensive gap between what a business is worth on paper and what a buyer will pay. It starts long before the sale and compounds every year it remains unaddressed.

## The Discount Is Quantified

Succession Thinking reports that owner-dependent businesses sell for 30 to 50 percent less than comparable owner-independent businesses. The same research shows founder-dependent companies achieve 3 to 4x EBITDA multiples at exit, while owner-independent businesses command 7 to 8x or higher. On a business with 3 million USD in EBITDA, the difference between a 4x multiple and an 8x multiple is 12 million USD, roughly 125 million SEK at current exchange rates.

The discount does not only appear at the sale. It starts the moment the business becomes structurally reliant on one person. Every year that the owner is the sole decision-maker, the sole relationship holder and the sole problem-solver is a year in which the business's transferable value declines relative to its reported value.

## What Buyers Look For

Sophisticated buyers assess five dimensions of owner dependency before naming a multiple. Per Succession Thinking's framework, the assessment covers leadership risk, whether strategy and culture depend on a single individual, personnel risk, whether the team can execute independently, customer risk, whether client relationships belong to the business or to the owner, operational risk, whether processes are documented and repeatable, and reputational risk, whether the brand stands apart from the owner's personal identity.

Each dimension has a test. Leadership risk shows up in board minutes and strategic decisions. Personnel risk shows up in turnover rates and succession planning. Customer risk shows up in retention data at six, twelve and twenty-four months. Operational risk shows up in process documentation and the owner's ability to step back without disruption. Reputational risk shows up in the gap between the owner's name and the business's independent market presence.

The cumulative effect is a valuation haircut that reflects real risk, not negotiation tactics. A buyer who identifies concentrated dependency applies a lower multiple to hedge against the possibility that the business deteriorates after the owner leaves.

## The Axial Data on Deal Failures

The problem shows up in market data. Per Axial's 2026 lower-middle-market survey of 107 dealmakers, valuation expectations were the single biggest reason deals failed to close in 2025, cited by 28.3 percent of respondents, narrowly ahead of diligence findings at 24.5 percent. Macroeconomic uncertainty accounted for 20.8 percent and financing constraints for 17.9 percent.

Owner dependence appeared directly in the qualitative data. Vipin Singh of Murphy Business Sales identified owner dependence, alongside messy financials and seller fatigue, as among the reasons deals are dying or going on hold. Among deals that did not close, 48.7 percent went on hold rather than dying outright, reflecting a market in which buyers are pausing for readiness and alignment rather than walking away permanently.

The survey data shows a cautious seller environment. Per the same Axial report, 61.5 percent of advisors described seller sentiment as opportunistic but cautious, while 19.2 percent characterized sellers as eager, up from 10.5 percent in mid-2025. Advisors expect more engagement activity in 2026, with 77.9 percent anticipating a year-over-year increase, while 70.5 percent of dealmakers expressed confidence that 2026 will be stronger than 2025. The pipeline is growing. The readiness gap remains the bottleneck.

## The Hidden Cost Beyond the Sale

The discount is not the only cost. Owner dependency produces four additional penalties that compound throughout the holding period. First, capital access suffers. Banks and lenders assess risk before committing funds, and an owner-dependent business is a higher-risk lending proposition. The result is higher interest rates, tighter covenants and lower borrowing capacity.

Second, strategic optionality disappears. An owner who cannot step back without the business suffering has no real options. He cannot take on an investor without accepting structural changes. He cannot take extended leave. He cannot respond to an unexpected opportunity or a health crisis because the business requires his daily presence.

Third, talent quality deteriorates. High-calibre senior operators can tell when decision-making is centralised in one person. They do not stay, because there is no room to lead. The businesses that retain the best people are the ones where accountability is genuinely distributed.

Fourth, the sale process itself becomes longer and more expensive. Buyers routinely require the selling owner to remain for one to three years post-acquisition under earnout structures that dilute the actual return. The earned price is not the agreed price.

## The Timeline Requirement

Owner dependency is a business improvement project, not an exit-only exercise. Per Succession Thinking's research, twelve to eighteen months is typically insufficient to build genuine owner independence. The owners who achieve premium multiples typically start the structural work three to five years before the sale. That timeline applies whether the goal is a sale, a succession or simply a business that runs well without the owner's daily involvement.

The work itself is disciplined and gradual. Delegate authority, not tasks. Document the processes that affect revenue, delivery and cash. Introduce account managers to key customers. Test whether the business can run without daily owner input, and fix the gaps. Repeat the test. Build a management team that can make decisions independently, and give them the data to justify those decisions.

The lead indicators matter more than the lag indicators. Revenue and profit tell you what happened. Customer retention data, management depth, process documentation and decision-making distribution tell you what the business is worth to someone else.

## Summary

The owner-dependency discount is real and it compounds. Succession Thinking reports that owner-dependent businesses sell for 30 to 50 percent less, with founder-dependent companies achieving 3 to 4x multiples versus 7 to 8x for owner-independent businesses. Per Axial's 2026 survey, valuation expectations were the top reason deals failed to close, cited by 28.3 percent of respondents, while owner dependence appeared directly in the qualitative data. Buyers assess leadership, personnel, customer, operational and reputational risk before naming a multiple. The timeline for removing owner dependency is three to five years, not one to two. The owners who achieve the best outcomes are the ones who build an owner-independent business long before they have a buyer in mind.
