## The Multiple Now Carries an AI Term

Mid-market exit multiples stopped being a pure function of revenue growth and margin in 2026. Buyers now price an AI term into every bid: how much of the cost base automation already compresses, and how fast the acquirer can compress the rest. Sellers who cannot show that term leave money on the table before the process starts.

The data supports the shift. Private SaaS companies trade at a median 22.4x EBITDA, while public software sits around 12.7x. Data infrastructure companies command 24.4x and DevOps platforms 36.5x, and the differentiator in every case is demonstrated AI deployment inside the operations.

The coefficient is not hypothetical. An industry survey counts 288 private equity platforms with 74 percent AI adoption, and the thesis now reads as an exit multiple with an AI coefficient. Buyers built value-creation playbooks around AI, and those playbooks price the target before the letter of intent.

## What the Data Shows

The multiple history frames the current market. EBITDA multiples ran 15 to 20x from 2015 to 2019, peaked at 39.9x in the second half of 2022, and corrected into a 17 to 22x band through 2023 and 2024.

The second half of 2025 normalized toward 26.1x as operating scale improved across the market. The AI premium shows in the sector spread. Companies that demonstrate effective AI adoption outprice their peers in the same category, with data infrastructure at 24.4x and DevOps at 36.5x against the 22.4x private median.

Buyers pay the premium because automation converts a variable human cost into a fixed software cost. EBITDA quality changes when the cost base stops scaling with revenue, and buyers underwrite the new quality with higher multiples. The multiple no longer prices the business as it is; it prices the business as the buyer will run it.

## Why Buyers Price Automation

Buyers price automation because it changes earnings durability. A company where software handles the repetitive workflow keeps its margin when revenue doubles, while a labor-dependent competitor re-hires at every step. The durable operator carries less execution risk, and the multiple discounts that risk.

The earnings quality shift is structural. AI adoption replaces marginal human cost with model cost, which declines per unit as volume grows. The resulting EBITDA is more predictable, and predictability is what multiple buyers actually purchase.

The value-creation playbook confirms the direction. PE operating partners now treat AI as an EBITDA driver: process automation, document intelligence, and workflow systems that cut cost before the first synergies. A target that already runs those systems sells into a buyer's plan instead of into a buyer's cost case.

The coefficient also sits inside the diligence model. Buyers model the automation runway, the number of quarters before the acquirer's stack replaces the seller's stack, and the margin delta between the two states. Sellers who shorten that runway with documented automation capture a higher entry multiple, because the buyer underwrites less integration risk.

## What Sellers Must Prove

The sell-side AI audit now decides the range. Buyers ask three questions: which processes automation covers, what the automation saves per quarter, and whether the stack survives the transition. Sellers who answer with documentation get the top of the band; sellers who answer with anecdotes get the bottom.

The evidence base is concrete. Model deployment logs, automation coverage rates, headcount-per-revenue ratios, and the delta between automated and manual process cost form the proof set. Buyers integrate this data into the diligence model, and the model produces the multiple.

The questions founders ask on Reddit reflect the same gap. Threads ask whether selling AI automation still works, what actually makes money in 2026, and whether the effort pays off. The answer in the exit market is the same as in the services market: documented, measurable automation carries value, and undifferentiated claims carry none.

## The SEK Math

The multiple effect converts directly into SEK.

Example: A mid-market company runs 60 MSEK revenue with a 20 percent EBITDA margin, producing 12 MSEK EBITDA. AI adoption compresses the cost base by 10 percentage points of revenue, lifting the margin to 30 percent and EBITDA to 18 MSEK.

At 7x, the multiple applies to the compressed base: 126 MSEK against 84 MSEK before automation. The 42 MSEK difference carries no extra revenue and no extra headcount, it is pure margin conversion. Sellers who automate before the process capture that conversion at the full multiple.

The compounding continues at exit. A buyer underwriting a 7x entry and an 11x exit on the same 18 MSEK base prices the asset at 198 MSEK, and the automation premium funds the acquisition debt. Sellers who leave the conversion to the buyer sell the upside at the entry multiple.

The margin conversion also changes the debt capacity. Lenders underwrite asset-based facilities against EBITDA, and an 18 MSEK base supports roughly twice the facility of a 12 MSEK base at the same gearing ratio. The automation premium therefore finances the acquisition twice: once in the equity price, once in the credit stack.

Sellers who document the automation runway also shorten the diligence timeline. A proof set built before the process compresses a six-month diligence into four, which reduces the odds of a broken deal and the cost of the sale. The coefficient pays in process terms as well as price terms.

## The Coefficient Rewrites Deal Structure

The AI premium changes more than the multiple, it changes the contract. Earnouts now carry automation milestones: churn targets after migration, margin floors at quarter twelve, and headcount-to-revenue ratios that hold after the founder leaves. Sellers who cannot commit to those numbers trade the premium for a lower cash price.

Buyers structure holdbacks around the stack. A portion of the purchase price stays in escrow until the model layer transfers, the data pipelines replicate, and the automation survives the first quarter under new ownership. The holdback size tracks the documentation quality, which gives sellers a direct financial reason to build the evidence base before the process.

Working capital mechanics shift as well. Automated companies run lower receivables because invoicing, collections, and dunning run on systems, and the net working capital adjustment shrinks. Buyers spend less diligence time on the balance sheet and more on the margin structure the automation creates.

## Why Adoption Is Uneven

The AI coefficient rewards operators who adopted early, and the gap between adopters and laggards widens every quarter. A company that automated customer onboarding in 2024 runs the same revenue with a cost base that is 8 to 12 percent lighter than a manual competitor. The margin difference compounds into the multiple difference at exit.

The unevenness has structural causes. Mid-market companies without a technical owner run pilots that die in the pilot phase, while companies with an engineering function deploy into production and measure the savings. The evidence base decides the multiple, and the evidence base requires measurement discipline that manual operators never built.

Sellers on the laggard side face a binary choice. They can automate before the process and sell into the premium, or they can sell the manual business and let the buyer capture the conversion. The first path adds more than 40 MSEK of value on a 60 MSEK revenue base at mid-market multiples; the second path hands that value to the acquirer.

## Edge Cases

The AI coefficient fails in defined conditions, and sellers should test them before relying on the premium.

One-off automation inflates the multiple. A company that automated a single workflow with a fragile script shows a margin spike that diligence finds non-recurring, and the correction lands the valuation at the pre-automation band. The premium requires breadth, not a demo.

Founder-dependent stacks break the transfer. When the automation runs on the founder's laptop and the model configuration lives in one engineer's head, the buyer discounts the EBITDA by the retention risk. The stack must survive the founder's departure.

Buyer integration risk cuts both ways. Acquirers who run a different stack plan to replace the target's automation, and the replacement cost lands in the purchase price. Sellers who document their stack as replaceable or compatible protect the multiple; sellers who built a locked-in custom system face the discount.

The definition of automation matters. Buyers discount bolt-on tools that sit outside the core workflow, because the acquirer replaces those tools in the first integration sprint. Automation embedded in the product, the billing system, and the fulfillment flow survives the transition, and only that automation carries the premium.

The market cycle matters. Multiples that run 26.1x in a normalized market compress when capital tightens, and the AI premium narrows with them. Automation still improves the absolute price, but sellers who time the market on the coefficient alone carry cycle risk.

## Summary

The AI coefficient is now a permanent term in mid-market exit multiples, and it prices demonstrated automation, earnings durability, and a transferable stack. The SEK math is direct: a ten-point margin conversion at 60 MSEK revenue adds 42 MSEK of value before the multiple applies. Sellers who document the automation, prove the savings, and make the stack transferable capture the premium, and sellers who leave the conversion to the buyer sell it at the entry multiple.
