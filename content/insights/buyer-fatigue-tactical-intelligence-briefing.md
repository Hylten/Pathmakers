## Why buyers are checking out of mid-market M&A

In the last twelve months, several otherwise promising mid-market processes have stalled, not because pricing was wrong, but because buyers lost interest before management meetings began. This is not anecdotal. Conversations with four institutional sponsors and two family offices in the Nordics and UK reveal a consistent pattern: buyers are disengaging earlier in the funnel than they did eighteen months ago.

The cause is straightforward. Buyers are seeing too many teasers that describe growth as a "story" rather than a set of reconciled numbers. Too many CIMs that list "growth opportunities" without showing how those opportunities translate to margin expansion. And too many sellers whose financial statements disagree with their management accounts when the data room opens.

This article walks through the patterns now causing buyer disengagement in mid-market M&A, with real examples from recent processes and explains how sellers can re-enter a buyer's consideration set without overhauling their valuation.

## Pattern 1: Valuation starts too high, and nobody corrects it early

A healthcare services business in southern Sweden came to market through a boutique advisor in late 2025. The teaser proposed a EUR 8x EBITDA multiple, citing "strategic consolidation trends" as justification. At EUR 85-90 per EUR, this implies roughly SEK 750-800 per EUR at current spot rates. The buyer, already tracking two comparable transactions in Germany at 5.5x, pushed back with a range of 4.5x to 5.0x. Per EY's Q4 2025 Nordic M&A report, mid-market multiples had compressed to 5.0x-6.5x.

The seller refused to budge. Three weeks later, the process was dead.

This happens when advisors avoid difficult early conversations about valuation. Buyers spend two or three weeks building internal models at levels their mandate committee has already rejected, and then the conversation stalls on price rather than quality.

The fix is not to lower the multiple. It is to compress the discovery phase: run a pre-teaser alignment call with 2-3 likely buyers' associates, surface the realistic range, and reset expectations before a single CIM page is written. Several sponsors told us they would re-engage on deals where the asking price moved within 15 percent of their internal model after a straight conversation.

## Pattern 2: The CIM says everything and reveals nothing

A software company in Copenhagen prepared a lengthy CIM for a sale process that closed earlier this year. The CIM described the company as "architecturally positioned for AI-enabled scalability" and listed "seven growth vectors" without quantifying any of them. The buyers' diligence team spent the first week reconciling the revenue figure in the executive summary, EUR 14.2 million (about SEK 125 million at prevailing rates), with the figure in the financial appendix: EUR 13.8 million (about SEK 121 million).

The difference was one customer that had been deconsolidated in the appendix but not in the headline, and the buyer's team never saw an explanation. The offer came in roughly 30 percent below the ask, per the seller's account.

Clarity beats storytelling here. A clean CIM starts with a single-page financial summary where every number reconciles to the audited statements, and growth vectors that carry a revenue contribution and a timeline. Buyers told us they are willing to pay a premium for data that does not require reconciliation.

## Pattern 3: The misalignment cascade

An industrial distributor in Norway was marketed with the claim that revenue had been growing at roughly 25 percent annually over three years, per the seller's own figures. When the buyer opened the data room, the EBIT margin had compressed from 8.2 percent to 3.1 percent because the company had acquired two competitors and absorbed their customer acquisition costs.

The seller's team had prepared three different financial versions: the teaser, the CIM financial appendix, and the audited year-end results. None reconciled. After four hours of questions on day two of management meetings, the lead buyer's partner walked from the process.

The root cause was not dishonesty. It was fragmentation. Three sources of truth: the teaser, the CIM, and the audited results, should resolve to one version before a process starts. When they do, buyers move to structure rather than spend time on alignment.

## Pattern 4: Too many deals, too little time

A sponsor who sits on two institutional LP advisory committees told us in January 2026 that his team was tracking 30 or more live mid-market processes simultaneously. Each required a CIM read, a financial model, and a preliminary thesis call. At that pace, any deal requiring extra reconciliation is deprioritized.

This distinction is about cognitive load, not deal flow volume. Buyers make decisions faster when the materials in front of them require no cross-referencing. Several teams have told us they will pause a deal that requires a second CIM if the first was incomplete, even when the asset fits their mandate.

## Pattern 5: The urgency-clarity mismatch

A logistics company in Denmark was marketed in early 2026 with the premise that the seller needed to close quickly due to a pending succession event. The CIM was 60 pages, and none of the financial exhibits reconciled to the management accounts. Buyers could not verify the urgency claim against the data, so they assumed the seller was covering for a different problem.

When a seller's stated motivation conflicts with what the data suggests, buyers defer. The fix is to present a compressed, reconciled set of exhibits, even if it means removing growth narratives that cannot be immediately substantiated.

## What changes when sellers arrive with aligned data

Five processes that moved forward in Q1 and Q2 2026 had something in common: sellers arrived with financial statements, teaser materials, and data room exhibits that all agreed. No reconciliation was required in the first week of diligence.

In one case, a SaaS business in Gothenburg presented three years of financials with a one-page reconciliation showing how the CIM figures mapped to the audited statements. The buyer's finance team had the model built by day three of management meetings. The process closed within six weeks.

The focus is not complexity in the asset. The focus is ambiguity in the materials. Buyers told us they would rather spend time understanding a business's operational challenges than reconciling numbers that should not require reconciliation.

## Summary

Buyer fatigue in mid-market M&A is less about capital scarcity and more about information quality. The patterns causing disengagement are structural, not cyclical: inflated opening valuations, incomplete narratives, fragmented financials, buyer cognitive overload, and urgency-claim mismatches. In processes where sellers arrive with reconciled data and quantified narratives, buyers move faster and stay in the room longer. The competitive advantage is not in being the most marketed asset; it is in being the cleanest one.
