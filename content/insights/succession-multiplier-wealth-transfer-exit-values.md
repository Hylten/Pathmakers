The wealth transfer is rewriting mid-market exit values. Owners in their sixties and seventies face succession without successors, and the demographic wave concentrates transfer supply in a measurable window. The sellers who prepare capture a structural premium; the sellers who wait accept a discount.

The multiplier works through timing. A business sold with two years of preparation, clean books, and a documented growth path commands a multiple that a distressed succession sale cannot. The difference is not market conditions; it is readiness.

This analysis examines the multiplier's mechanics: succession pressure, buyer competition, preparation economics, and the window that separates premium exits from forced ones.

## The Succession Wave

The succession wave is scheduled. The post-war generation passes ownership over two decades, with the Nordic concentration peaking in the current window. Each owner faces the same binary: transfer within the family or sell to a prepared buyer.

The family transfer path narrows. Successors are absent, uninterested, or unprepared. The sale path widens because the volume attracts buyer capital.

The combination produces a concentrated supply of motivated sellers.

The concentration is the multiplier's fuel. A seller prepared before the wave peaks negotiates from strength. A seller entering the market at peak supply negotiates against the calendar.

## Buyer Competition

The wealth transfer attracts every buyer class. Strategic acquirers seek platforms. Private equity funds deploy committed capital.

Independent sponsors execute search strategies. The buyer pool deepens exactly when the seller pool widens.

The competition benefits the prepared seller. Multiple qualified buyers produce a competitive process, and a competitive process produces premium multiples. The seller who enters with a documented story captures the spread.

The unprepared seller loses the competition. Buyers discount what they cannot verify. A business with messy books, owner-dependent revenue, and thin management attracts fewer bids at lower multiples.

The discount is the price of unreadiness.

## Preparation Economics

Preparation converts into multiple points. Clean financials, a documented growth path, and a management team that operates without the owner remove the buyer's risk. The risk reduction prices directly into the offer.

The preparation window is eighteen to twenty-four months. Owners who begin early execute the full sequence: financial normalization, management development, buyer qualification, process design. Owners who begin late accept the discount.

The economics are asymmetric. The preparation cost is a fraction of the value captured. A multiple point on a mid-market exit exceeds the advisory cost by orders of magnitude.

The seller who treats preparation as an investment, not an expense, captures the multiplier.

## The Exit Multiplier

The multiplier is the difference between a prepared exit and a forced one. The prepared seller achieves a competitive process, premium multiples, and terms that preserve optionality. The forced seller accepts a single-bid process, discounted multiples, and terms set by the buyer.

The difference compounds across the transaction. A premium multiple on a growing business produces an exit value that funds the next generation. A discounted multiple on a constrained business produces a transfer that resets the family's position.

The succession context adds the emotional dimension. The prepared exit is a planned transition, a stewardship act. The forced exit is a loss event, a failure of timing.

The multiplier rewards the planned path.

## The Window

The window is measurable but closing. The succession wave peaks in the current period, and buyer competition concentrates with it. The sellers who prepare now operate at the peak; the sellers who wait face the declining phase.

The institutional buyer with committed capital and origination infrastructure captures the window at source. The prepared seller meets that buyer on equal terms. The unprepared seller meets the same buyer from a deficit.

The window belongs to the prepared on both sides. The seller who prepares captures the premium, and the buyer who prepares captures the access. The multiplier rewards readiness as a system.

## Edge Cases

The multiplier carries identifiable risks. The first is overpreparation: spending on advisory before the business justifies it. The mitigation is staged preparation tied to buyer interest.

The second is valuation anchoring: expecting pre-wave multiples in a post-wave market.

The third is process fatigue: a long preparation window that outlasts buyer appetite. The mitigation is disciplined process design with clear timelines. The fourth is management dependence: a team that cannot operate without the owner.

The fifth is successor conflict: family members with competing claims that derail the process. The mitigation is early governance resolution. Each scenario is manageable with structure and timing.

## Summary

The wealth transfer multiplies mid-market exit values for the prepared. Succession pressure concentrates supply, buyer competition deepens demand, and preparation converts into multiple points. The seller who executes the full sequence captures the premium.

The window is scheduled and measurable. Preparation is the difference between a planned transition and a forced transfer. The sellers who treat readiness as a system capture the multiplier, and the buyers who build origination infrastructure capture the access.
