The seller who enters the auction without a Quality of Earnings analysis hands the buyer the negotiation advantage. The buyer's due diligence discovers the adjustments, the normalization questions, and the one-time charges that the seller omitted from the teaser. Each discovery becomes a bid reduction, and the seller who could have resolved the issues before the process loses the premium that preparation creates.

Per CT Acquisitions' 2026 exit preparation playbook, a seller-side QoE completed four to six months before market adds one to two turns to the exit multiple, and the cost of the analysis typically pays for itself tenfold. The mechanism is straightforward: the buyer's diligence time shrinks, the price discovery accelerates, and the information asymmetry that normally favors the buyer shifts back to the prepared seller.

## The Information Asymmetry That Sets the Multiple

A buyer enters diligence with three questions that determine the price. First, what is the true recurring revenue, stripped of one-time clients and deferred contracts. Second, what are the real operating expenses, normalized for owner add-backs and non-recurring costs. Third, what liabilities are not yet on the balance sheet, from pending litigation to environmental exposure to key-person dependency.

The seller who answers those questions in the teaser, with documentation, holds the positional advantage. The seller who lets the buyer discover them in diligence holds nothing. Per Boxwood Partners' Ultimate Sell-Side QoE Guide, the average QoE uncovers two to four material adjustments that were not disclosed to buyers, and each adjustment reduces the offer by five to fifteen percent of its value.

The multiplier effect comes from the bidder count. A QoE that resolves the accounting questions in the first week of diligence keeps more bidders in the process for longer, and the competition between remaining bidders drives the multiple up. A QoE that arrives late or incomplete drops bidders out as the diligence deadline tightens, and the multiple compresses.

## The Preparation Timeline

The QoE is not a pre-auction checklist. It is a structural process that reshapes the financial presentation over four to six months. Per CT Acquisitions, the timeline runs in four phases.

Phase one runs weeks one through eight and builds the QoE workpaper structure. The seller provides three years of financial statements, tax filings, and audit letters. The QoE team maps every line item and flags the adjustments that require documentation.

Phase two runs weeks nine through sixteen and executes the diligence. Bank statements are reconciled to the financial statements, revenue recognition is tested across contracts, and expense classifications are validated against vendor invoices. The seller provides supporting documentation for every add-back, and the QoE team tests each item for defensibility.

Phase three runs weeks seventeen through twenty and resolves the findings. The seller corrects the financial presentation, removes the non-recurring charges, and normalizes the expense base. The QoE report documents every adjustment with supporting evidence that the buyer can verify independently.

Phase four runs weeks twenty-one through twenty-four and packages the output for the market. The cleaned financials become the teaser basis, the QoE report becomes the diligence shortcut, and the normalized earnings become the valuation anchor.

The four-phase timeline means the QoE that arrives after the teaser is worthless. By week four, the buyer has already formed a price hypothesis, and the QoE either validates or contradicts it. A contradiction triggers a renegotiation, and a renegotiation is a bid reduction.

## The Adjustments That Move the Multiple

The QoE team identifies three categories of adjustment that expand the reported earnings and compress the buyer's perceived risk. First, the owner discretionary add-backs: the personal vehicle expenses, the family salaries, the non-recurring consulting fees that the owner treated as operating costs.

Per KMCO's analysis of sell-side QoE outcomes, the average owner add-back represents twelve to eighteen percent of reported EBITDA across lower-middle-market companies. The adjustment is defensible when documented with vendor invoices and board approvals, and it is indefensible when presented as a verbal assertion in the teaser.

Second, the non-recurring revenue items: the one-time license fee, the litigation settlement, the contract termination payment that inflated the revenue peak. The QoE strips these from the revenue line and normalizes to the recurring base.

Third, the liability adjustments: the pending lawsuit, the warranty exposure, the lease termination that has not yet been accrued. The QoE quantifies these and presents them as adjustments to the working capital floor, not as post-closing surprises.

## The Multiple Expansion Mechanics

The buyer's multiple on the cleaned EBITDA is not the seller's exit value. The exit value is the cleaned EBITDA multiplied by the multiple, minus the buyer's diligence cost. The diligence cost is not just the hours spent. The diligence cost is the bidder attrition that occurs when the financial presentation is unclear.

At an 8x multiple, the 90,000 SEK cost of a QoE that added 220,000 SEK of normalized EBITDA generates a 1.76 million SEK expansion in valuation, per CT Acquisitions' case study data. The arithmetic is mechanical: twenty-two thousand SEK of EBITDA expansion times eight is 176,000 SEK of enterprise value, and the QoE cost of 90,000 SEK leaves a 86,000 SEK net gain before the buyer's own diligence cost is deducted.

The gain multiplies when the QoE keeps bidders in the process. A buyer that would have dropped out after week three of diligence because of unclear financials stays through week six because the QoE resolved the questions. The additional bidder drives the multiple from the first-bidder level to the competitive level, and the QoE cost has now paid for itself twice over.

## The Risk Premium Elimination

The seller's QoE also removes the risk premium that buyers bake into their offers. A buyer facing unclear financials adds a ten to twenty percent risk buffer to the bid, reflecting the uncertainty about the true earnings and the potential for post-closing adjustments.

The prepared seller removes that buffer. The QoE presents the financials with documentation, the adjustments with evidence, and the earnings normalization with audit trail. The buyer has no reason to add the risk buffer, and the full multiple applies to the cleaned earnings.

Per KMCO's QoE analysis, sellers with a pre-auction QoE achieve offers at the top of their expected range in 73 percent of cases, compared to 31 percent for sellers without preparation. The difference is the risk premium elimination: the buyer who sees clean numbers has no justification for a discount, and the seller who provided the clean numbers has no justification for accepting one.

## The Structural Position

The QoE is a structural position, not a tactical maneuver. The seller who schedules it four to six months before the auction has the time to resolve the adjustments, document the add-backs, and present the cleaned earnings as the teaser basis.

The seller who schedules it after the teaser is written is performing damage control, and the buyer who sees the late QoE treats it as a red flag, not a reassurance. The timing is the structural decision, and the multiple outcome follows from it.

## Summary

A sell-side QoE completed four to six months before market adds one to two turns to the exit multiple. The 90,000 SEK cost generated 1.76 million SEK of valuation expansion at an 8x multiple, per CT Acquisitions case study data. The QoE resolves information asymmetry, removes the buyer's risk premium, and keeps bidders in the process through diligence. The seller who treats the QoE as a structural position, not a checklist, enters the auction with the positional advantage. The seller who skips it negotiates from the liability side of the table.
