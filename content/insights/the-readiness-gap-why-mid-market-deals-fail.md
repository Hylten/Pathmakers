A founder tells his advisor he is ready to sell. Six months later the process is dead, the data room was never completed, and the buyer walked. The founder was sincere. The company was not ready. The gap between those two facts is where most mid-market value is lost, and almost no one plans for it.

The deal community measures itself by closed transactions. The more useful metric is the deals that never reached a signed term sheet because the seller was not prepared for the scrutiny a buyer brings. This is a structural feature of mid-market M&A, not a series of unfortunate exceptions.

## Intent Is Not Readiness

A founder decides to sell for a reason: fatigue, age, a partner dispute, a strategic pivot. The decision is emotional and sudden. The company, however, was built to operate, not to be examined. Financials live in a founder's head, contracts are unsigned or verbal, and key relationships have no documentation.

Our assessment is that the average mid-market business needs nine to twelve months of preparation before it can survive institutional diligence without value leakage. The founder who expects a clean exit in two quarters has confused intent with condition. The buyer's diligence will surface every gap the founder stopped seeing years ago.

This is not a criticism of founders. It is a description of a mismatch. The seller optimises for running the business; the buyer optimises for verifying it. The readiness gap is the distance between those two modes, and only deliberate work closes it.

## The Diligence Wall

Institutional buyers run a diligence process that a founder has never experienced. Quality of earnings reports reconstruct the P&L under standard definitions. Legal review tests every contract for change-of-control clauses. Tax review examines structures the founder assumed were fine. Each step exposes a weakness that was invisible in the daily operation.

The European Central Bank's reports on small and medium enterprise financing note that information asymmetry between borrower and lender is a persistent friction in mid-market transactions. The same asymmetry exists between seller and buyer, and it widens exactly when the seller is least prepared to close it. The buyer discounts for every unknown, and the discount compounds.

According to advisory market colour reported by Reuters and the major accounting networks, a meaningful share of mid-market deals that enter exclusivity fail to close, and the dominant cause is diligence-derived repricing or breakdown rather than price disagreement at the outset. The number varies by year and sector, but the direction is stable: most failures are discovered, not negotiated.

## The Psychology of the Stalled Process

The founder who stalls is rarely lazy. He is protecting a life's work from a stranger's spreadsheet. When the buyer's team requests three years of customer-level margin data, the founder experiences it as an accusation, not a routine step. The emotional load of selling a company is underestimated by every model that treats M&A as a financial transaction.

Pathmakers' work with owners shows that the deals which closed smoothly were the ones where the founder had reconciled the sale emotionally before the process began. The deals that dragged were the ones where the founder was still deciding, week by week, whether to let go. No advisor can compensate for an owner who has not made peace with the exit.

This is why readiness has a psychological dimension that financial preparation alone does not solve. The acquirer who ignores it will misread a hesitant seller as a difficult negotiator and walk from a good asset.

## What Acquirers Should Do Differently

The buyer who treats readiness as the seller's problem inherits a broken process. The buyer who builds readiness into origination wins deals that never reach a competitive auction. The mechanism is simple: engage the seller early, fund the preparation, and structure the close around the timeline the company actually needs.

Roials Capital's origination approach reaches owners before they list, which means the readiness gap is visible from the first conversation. A principal who knows the founder needs a year to prepare can offer that year as part of the relationship, instead of demanding a data room that does not yet exist. This is origination as enablement, not extraction.

The practical steps are concrete. Fund a quality-of-earnings review before exclusivity so the founder sees his own numbers the way the buyer will. Map the key-person dependency and build a transition plan. Document the verbal contracts. Each step reduces the discount the buyer will otherwise impose for uncertainty.

## The Compounding Advantage of Prepared Sellers

A seller who enters the market prepared commands a different conversation. The buyer spends time on price and strategic fit, not on discovering whether the business is real. The data room is complete, the story is coherent, and the founder can answer the hard questions without defensiveness.

Our experience is that prepared sellers close faster and at higher multiples than comparable unprepared businesses, because the buyer prices certainty, not potential. The readiness investment, typically a fraction of the transaction value, returns many times over in avoided leakage and retained multiple.

The systemic implication is that the mid-market lacks a readiness infrastructure. Advisors sell processes; few sell preparation. The firms that supply preparation as a first step, years before a sale, capture the best assets on the best terms.

## Summary

Most mid-market deals fail in diligence, not in negotiation, because of the readiness gap between a founder's intent to sell and the company's actual condition. Intent is not readiness, the diligence wall exposes every operational gap, and the founder's psychology determines whether the process holds. Acquirers who build readiness into origination, fund preparation early and treat the seller as a partner capture assets that never reach a competitive auction. Preparation is the cheapest multiple protector in mid-market M&A.
